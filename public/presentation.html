Patch — Right to Repair

The thinking behind it

How we went from a policy intent — every social-housing tenant has a right to a timely repair — to a working, agentic service. This page is the user-centred design and product reasoning underneath the prototype.

Open the prototype See the dispatcher view

One sentence: we designed a single service that lets a resident report and track a repair in plain English, and lets a council dispatcher approve an AI agent's live, whole-day re-balancing of the work — while quietly spotting building-level problems a one-ticket-at-a-time process can't.

Section 1Discovery: the problem we found

Social-housing repairs are where policy meets people's homes. After Awaab Ishak's death and the strengthening of the law around damp and mould, the duty is clear — but the day-to-day reality is a queue. We ran a short, focused discovery against three lived realities.

Resident

“I reported it. Then silence.”

People don't know how urgent the council thinks their problem is, what happens next, or when. Uncertainty reads as not being taken seriously.

Dispatcher

“I book one job and knock three.”

Slotting a new emergency into a full diary has knock-on effects the dispatcher can't see until tomorrow. They optimise one ticket because that's all the tooling shows them.

Housing officer

“The damp keeps coming back.”

Each flat is fixed in isolation. Nobody is positioned to notice that five reports in one block over a month point to one structural cause.

!

The core insight: the work isn't a list of tickets, it's a system — a finite set of engineers, a housing stock with shared fabric, and residents who need to trust the process. Triaging one ticket at a time is structurally blind to all three.

Section 3User needs & “How might we”

We wrote needs as testable statements, then turned each into a design question.

As a resident I need to know how seriously my repair is being taken and what happens next, so that I can trust the council without having to chase.
As a dispatcher I need to see the whole day's consequences before I commit a booking, so that I can fit urgent work in without quietly breaching another tenant's SLA.
As a housing officer I need to see problems that span homes, so that I can fix a cause once instead of a symptom five times.

How might we…

  • …make an AI agent feel like a trustworthy colleague the dispatcher approves, not an automation that acts behind them?
  • …reason across the whole queue and the whole building, while keeping every individual job card honest and simple?
  • …keep the front door open and friendly while keeping junk, off-topic reports and unsafe files out?

Section 4Three design responses

Each feature is a direct answer to a need above. The thread running through all three: the agent does the thinking; the human keeps the decision.

4.1 — Cross-job schedule optimisation

Answers the dispatcher's “I book one job and knock three.”

The agent reasons over the entire active queue, not one job in isolation. When a higher-priority repair needs an earlier slot, it can propose bringing it forward by moving one lower-priority booked job — but only ever as a clear before/after trade-off, and only when the moved job stays within its own SLA. A safer, non-disruptive option is always shown alongside. Nothing changes until the dispatcher approves.

What the dispatcher sees

Re-balanced plan — brings this job forward to Fri, 08:00–10:00.

To do this, the agent moves one lower-priority job:

Routine Fri, 08:00–10:00 → Sat, 08:00–10:00

✓ Still within SLA by 26 days

…or book a free slot and move no one. The dispatcher chooses; the agent never books behind them.

4.2 — Estate intelligence

Damp & mould

Maple Court

3 reports · 3 homes · within 22 days

Three damp reports across three flats in one block in a few weeks points to a shared cause — likely failed damp-proofing, guttering or communal ventilation — rather than three coincidences.

Recommended: flag the block for a structural / damp survey.

Answers the officer's “the damp keeps coming back.”

A background process looks for patterns across reports from the same building and flags a possible shared cause — with its reasoning and a recommended action — as something distinct from the individual job cards.

!

Kept to the Ops view only. Building-level clusters are never shown on the public dashboard — surfacing “these specific homes have damp” could expose residents before anything is confirmed.

4.3 — Trust at the front door: validation & scanning

Answers “keep the door friendly, keep junk out.”

  • Relevant photos. Screenshots and unrelated images are gently declined with a friendly ask to re-upload something that shows the repair.
  • Sufficient, on-topic text. Empty, spammy or off-topic submissions (“unhappy with my landlord”) get a helpful redirect — a nudge, not a hard error wall.
  • A visible safety step. Every file shows “Scanning for viruses and malware…”, then a clear pass — the ordinary, expected trust step, not a feature being shown off.

Section 5Agentic design principles

Four rules kept the AI on the right side of the line in every screen.

  1. Approve, don't automate

    The agent proposes; a human commits. It earns autonomy by being right, not by being given the keys.

  2. Show your working

    Every recommendation comes with its reasoning and the trade-off in plain English — including when it's honest about a breach it can't avoid.

  3. Safe by default

    There is always a non-disruptive option, and the agent will never move a job out of its own SLA to help another.

  4. One service, one set of data

    The resident, dispatcher and public views read and write the same source of truth, so what one side does shows up instantly on the others.

Section 6Safety, ethics & accessibility

Protecting residents

  • Building-level clusters stay internal — never published in a way that could identify a home.
  • The agent cannot trade one tenant's SLA away to serve another without a human seeing it.
  • Validation declines content kindly; it never blames or shames the person reporting.

Built to GDS

  • GOV.UK Design System patterns, palette and type — including this page.
  • Plain English, one thing per screen, no jargon, generous targets.
  • Keyboard-navigable, visible focus, semantic structure, status messages announced.

Section 7How we'd know it worked

Outcomes, not output. The public dashboard makes the council accountable to its own numbers.

↑ Repairs completed within SLA — the headline outcome.
↓ Time-to-acknowledge and resident chase contacts.
↓ Repeat reports per block — proof estate intelligence is fixing causes.
What we deliberately did and didn't build (alpha)
Built now — highest learning per effort Parked — needs real integration / data
Resident report → triage → live tracking Real auth, accounts and notifications
Whole-queue re-optimise with human approval Live engineer GPS, vans and stock systems
Estate clustering across reports Asset register & survey workflow integration
Friendly validation + simulated virus scan Production ML vision & a real AV engine

Section 8Where this goes next

  • Private beta: connect to a real repairs/asset system; replace simulated checks with production services.
  • Agent confidence: let the agent auto-book only the safest, unambiguous routine jobs once it has a track record — still visible, still reversible.
  • Estate intelligence loop: close the loop from “flag for survey” to a tracked structural workstream with budget.
  • Accessibility & assisted digital: test with assistive tech and offline/phone routes for residents who can't self-serve.

Section 9How this was made
